## *Active–Constructive–Interactive* (Chi, 2009) — concise summary

**Purpose of the paper**
The paper defines and differentiates three commonly used but poorly specified learning terms—*active, constructive, interactive*—by grounding them in **observable learner behaviors** and **underlying cognitive processes**. 

---

## Core framework

Chi proposes a **hierarchy of learning activities**:

* **Passive** — no overt engagement
* **Active** — doing something (e.g., highlighting, repeating)
* **Constructive** — generating new ideas beyond the given material (e.g., self-explaining)
* **Interactive** — jointly building knowledge through dialogue

This yields a testable hypothesis:

> **Interactive > Constructive > Active > Passive** in learning effectiveness 

---

## Key distinctions

**1. Observable behavior matters**
The framework classifies learning by *what learners do*, not by instructional method. 

**2. The hinge is knowledge generation**

* Active = engagement without new ideas
* Constructive = generation of new inferences
* Interactive = co-generation of new understanding

**3. Content quality determines category**
The same activity (e.g., note-taking, questioning) can be:

* Active (copying)
* Constructive (adding new meaning)
  depending on what is actually produced

---

## Underlying cognitive processes

* **Active → attending**
  (activate, encode, store information)

* **Constructive → creating**
  (infer, integrate, reorganize knowledge)

* **Interactive → joint creation**
  (build, challenge, refine ideas with others)

Constructive processes *subsume* active ones; interactive adds partner contributions. 

---

## Why interaction is strongest

Interactive learning improves outcomes because partners provide:

* corrective feedback
* alternative perspectives
* new information
* challenges that force deeper reasoning

This can produce **shared mental models** that neither learner would reach alone. 

---

## Evidence strategy

The paper validates the framework by:

1. **Pairwise comparisons** (e.g., constructive vs active)
2. **Equivalence tests** (same-type activities yield similar outcomes)
3. **Context sensitivity** (effect depends on how an activity is performed)

---

## Practical implication

You cannot judge a learning method by its label (e.g., “discussion,” “note-taking”).

You must ask:

* What is the learner *actually doing*?
* Are they **generating** ideas?
* Are they **building on others’ thinking**?

---

## Bottom line

Learning effectiveness tracks **cognitive transformation**, not visible activity.

* Engagement is necessary
* Generation is powerful
* Co-generation is most powerful

This is the structural claim the paper makes—and supports.
