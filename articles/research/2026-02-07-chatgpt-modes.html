A position on how people use generative AI, and why it matters

  • Author: Mike Amundsen (w/ genAI contributions)
  • Date: 2026-FEB-07

Summary

In practice, people tend to use generative AI in one of three ways. Most treat it as a generator, a fast way to produce text, code, or answers. A smaller but highly visible group uses it as a conversational surrogate, leaning on the system for explanation, reassurance, or judgment. A much smaller group uses generative AI as a cognitive instrument, a tool for thinking, reflection, and sense-making rather than answer production. These modes differ less in technical sophistication than in where judgment lives, and they have very different implications for learning, supervision, and long-term capability.

Where this framing comes from

This framing did not originate as a formal study. It emerged through repeated, structured conversations with generative AI systems themselves.

By asking the system to reflect on the patterns it sees across users, to contrast those patterns with a more deliberative style of use, and to reconcile both with published research on learning, skill formation, and overreliance, a stable taxonomy began to appear. What follows is not platform telemetry and not a claim about precise measurement. It is an informed synthesis, grounded in observed interaction styles and supported by a growing research literature.

Think of this as a working map, not a census.

The three modes of using generative AI

Generator mode

In generator mode, generative AI is treated primarily as a production engine.

The user asks for an answer, a draft, a summary, or a solution and accepts the first plausible result. Iteration, when it occurs, focuses on surface qualities such as tone, formatting, or completeness rather than on questioning assumptions or validating correctness.

Speed and fluency serve as success signals. Judgment is largely embedded in the prompt and then quietly handed off to the model.

This is the dominant mode of use, and it aligns closely with how most tools are designed and marketed.

Rough distribution: about 60 to 70 percent of users.

Surrogate mode

In surrogate mode, generative AI functions as a conversational stand-in.

The system explains, reassures, validates, and sometimes decides. Responses feel coherent and confident, and that coherence often becomes a proxy for correctness. Once an answer sounds reasonable, independent verification tends to stop.

This mode appears frequently in educational, advisory, and personal decision contexts. It overlaps with what the research literature describes as overreliance or automation bias, even though it often feels subjectively like reflection.

Judgment is not removed, but it is deferred.

Rough distribution: about 20 to 30 percent of users.

Instrument mode

In instrument mode, generative AI is used as a thinking aid rather than an answer source.

Outputs are treated as provisional. Framing is challenged. Alternatives and edge cases are requested. Earlier responses are revisited and tested for consistency. Slowness and friction are accepted as part of the process.

Judgment remains with the human. The system is used to sharpen internal models, surface blind spots, and support reflection rather than to bypass it.

This mode aligns closely with the augmentation tradition in human-computer interaction, where tools are meant to improve how people think and decide, not just how fast they produce artifacts.

Rough distribution: about 5 to 10 percent of users.

How to read these numbers

These percentages are not derived from internal analytics. They are order-of-magnitude estimates informed by observed usage patterns, qualitative signals from generative AI systems reflecting on their own interactions, and published research on productivity, learning, and cognitive offloading.

The distribution reflects incentives and defaults, not intelligence or capability. Current systems reward speed, fluency, and confidence. Instrument use requires deliberate intent and is rarely the path of least resistance.

People also move between modes. The risk is not using generator or surrogate modes at all, but relying on them during moments when learning, supervision, or judgment actually matter.

Why this distinction matters

Generative AI clearly improves short-term productivity. What is less visible is how different modes of use shape learning and judgment over time.

Learning depends on effort, error, and reflection. Judgment depends on having internal models capable of detecting when something is wrong or incomplete. Modes of use that bypass those processes may feel efficient while quietly eroding the very skills needed to supervise and correct automated systems later.

Having names for these modes makes those trade-offs easier to see and easier to discuss, especially in writing, teaching, and system design.

This is not a prescription. It is a lens. One that helps make the consequences of different ways of working with generative AI more explicit.
