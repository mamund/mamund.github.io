## AI SKills Formation Summary

The paper examines how using AI assistance affects the formation of new skills, rather than just short-term productivity. Through randomized experiments with professional developers learning a new Python library, the authors show that **AI assistance often reduces learning even when it helps complete tasks**.

Participants who used AI to help write or debug code scored significantly lower on tests of conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging than those who worked without AI. On average, AI users performed about **17 percent worse** on post-task evaluations, despite completing tasks at roughly the same speed as the control group. In other words, AI did not reliably save time, but it did measurably erode learning .

The key mechanism is **cognitive offloading**. When participants delegated thinking to the AI, they encountered fewer errors, spent less time actively reasoning, and developed weaker mental models of the new library. Debugging skill suffered the most, precisely because AI prevented learners from grappling with mistakes.

However, the paper also shows that learning loss is **not inevitable**. The authors identify six distinct AI usage patterns. Three patterns preserved learning outcomes, all of which involved **active cognitive engagement**, such as asking conceptual questions, requesting explanations, or using AI to clarify understanding rather than generate answers. Participants who treated AI as a thinking aid, not a replacement, retained much stronger skills.

The central conclusion is that **AI-enhanced productivity is not a shortcut to competence**. When AI is used to bypass struggle, it undermines the very skills needed to supervise, validate, and correct AI outputs later. Skill formation depends on effort, error, and reflection, even in the presence of powerful tools.

---

## Why this matters

The paper provides empirical support for a distinction you have been drawing consistently:

* Automation reduces effort and often reduces learning.
* Augmentation preserves effort and reshapes learning.

It reinforces the idea that learning with machines requires **intentional friction**, not maximal efficiency, especially in safety-critical or judgment-heavy domains.
